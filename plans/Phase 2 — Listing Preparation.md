Here is the full broker rulebook for Listing Preparation — every responsibility, rule, and decision a broker owns at this stage.

***

## The Listing Prep Gate: What Must Be True Before Phase 2 Starts

A broker will **not** begin listing prep until these conditions are met. This is a hard gate:

- Recast financials are finalized and the seller has signed off on the SDE/EBITDA figure
- Valuation range is agreed upon by seller (many sellers reject the first number — broker must get alignment before any document is created that contains a price)
- Listing agreement is fully executed with clear authorization to market the business
- Seller has disclosed all material facts (prior deal attempts, litigation, lease issues) in writing
- Engagement on deal structure is complete — asset vs. stock, seller note willingness, real estate decision

Nothing in Phase 2 can be built on a foundation the seller hasn't accepted. A CIM with a price the seller will later dispute destroys broker credibility with buyers.

***

## The CIM — Deep Rules

The CIM is the most legally and commercially consequential document in the deal. Brokers treat it with the rigor of a prospectus.

### Structure Rules

Every professional CIM has a defined section order. Deviation from the order is a signal of an inexperienced broker:

1. **Cover page** — business name (or code name if still anonymous), tagline, date, broker contact, confidentiality notice
2. **Disclaimer / confidentiality legend** — on every single page, not just the cover. Standard language: "This document is confidential and intended solely for the use of the party to whom it is addressed. Reproduction or distribution is prohibited." This is not decorative — it establishes the legal basis for remedies if a buyer leaks it.
3. **Executive summary** — 1 page maximum. The investment thesis in plain English: what the business does, why it's valuable, why now is the right time to buy, and the asking price. This page is what a buyer reads first and often last. If the exec summary doesn't land, the rest doesn't get read.
4. **Business overview** — history, founding story, business model, products/services, revenue streams, geographic footprint, competitive positioning
5. **Market & industry overview** — TAM narrative, industry tailwinds, competitive landscape (without naming competitors directly), why this business has a defensible position
6. **Operations** — day-to-day operational structure, facilities, technology stack, key processes, hours of operation, geographic reach. The goal is to answer: can a new owner run this?
7. **Team & org structure** — headcount by role, tenure of key employees, owner's current role and hours per week, transition plan overview. Any key-man risk must be addressed here, not hidden.
8. **Customer overview** — customer count, concentration breakdown (top customer as % of revenue), contract status, churn metrics if SaaS/subscription, geographic distribution. This section is where concentration risk lives and must be disclosed accurately.
9. **Financial summary** — 3-year recast P&L showing SDE or EBITDA, trailing twelve months, and current year projection. Presented as clean tables, not prose. Year-over-year growth rates called out explicitly.
10. **Valuation & asking price** — the methodology used (SDE multiple, EBITDA multiple, revenue multiple as a cross-check), the multiple applied, the resulting range, and the asking price. Brokers call out what's included (assets, inventory at cost, A/R if applicable) and what's excluded (cash, A/P).
11. **Deal structure** — asset vs. stock, proposed terms, seller financing availability, SBA eligibility statement, earnout structure if applicable
12. **Growth opportunities** — 3-5 specific, realistic levers a new owner could pull. Must be concrete, not generic ("expand to social media" is not a growth thesis). Examples: add a second location in an underserved zip code, hire a dedicated sales rep to go after commercial accounts, launch a recurring service tier.
13. **Reason for sale** — mandatory section. Buyers always ask. The narrative must be truthful and framed constructively. "Owner retirement" and "pursuing new ventures" are the two most accepted reasons. Anything that sounds evasive destroys deal momentum.
14. **Next steps / process** — how to move forward, NDA requirement, what happens after NDA execution (buyer packet, management call), timeline

### Content Rules

- **No identifiable information before NDA** — business name, address, owner name, specific customer names, and any other identifying detail must be removed from the version distributed pre-NDA. The pre-NDA version is called the "blind CIM" or "teaser package." The full CIM with identity revealed is sent only after NDA execution.
- **All financial figures must be recast figures** — never raw tax return numbers. The CIM presents the normalized, add-back-adjusted earnings, with an exhibit or footnote showing the bridge from tax return to recast.
- **No projections without clear labeling** — if forward revenue projections are included, they must be labeled "Management Projections" and the broker must add a disclaimer that actual results may differ. Many brokers avoid forward projections entirely because they create liability if the business underperforms post-close.
- **Photos must not identify the business** — interior operational photos are fine (showing clean facility, equipment, staff capacity); exterior shots with signage are not included until post-NDA.
- **Page count discipline** — a CIM for a sub-$1M business should be 15-25 pages. A $1M-$5M business, 25-40 pages. Longer is not better. Buyers are busy; a 60-page CIM for a $500K listing signals an inexperienced broker burying problems in volume.
- **Consistency rule** — every number in the CIM must reconcile back to the source document it came from. If the executive summary says TTM revenue is $1.2M, the financial summary table must also say $1.2M. Any internal inconsistency is a due diligence red flag.
- **Version control** — each CIM distributed must be watermarked with a buyer-specific identifier so that if it leaks, the source can be traced. This is standard practice and legally important.

## Teaser / Blind Profile — Deep Rules

The teaser is a 1-page document. Its only job is to get a qualified prospect to execute an NDA.

- **Must be truly anonymous** — not just name redacted. Industry, geography, and revenue figures must be calibrated so that a competitor or employee cannot identify the business from the description alone. For niche businesses in small markets, this is harder than it sounds. A broker must think adversarially: who could reverse-engineer this?
- **The hook is the financials** — buyers scan teasers for SDE or EBITDA first, asking price second, and industry/model third. The teaser must lead with the financial headline.
- **No contact information for the business** — all inquiries route to the broker. This is how the broker controls the process and maintains confidentiality.
- **Call to action is singular** — "Execute NDA to receive full CIM and financials." No ambiguity. No alternative paths.
- **Distribution tracking** — every teaser distribution is logged. Who received it, on what date, through what channel. If the deal later has a confidentiality breach, this log is the evidence trail.

### What a Teaser Contains (and Nothing More)

- Industry and general geography (state/region, not city)
- Revenue and SDE/EBITDA range (often rounded to nearest $50K to obscure exact figures)
- Asking price or asking price range
- One-sentence business model description
- 2-3 headline reasons to buy (recurring revenue, low competition, established brand in market)
- NDA requirement statement and broker contact

## Marketplace Listing Drafts — Deep Rules

Each marketplace has its own format requirements, audience characteristics, and strategic purpose. A broker doesn't post the same copy everywhere.

### Platform-Specific Strategy

| Platform | Primary Buyer Audience | Broker's Strategic Use |
|---|---|---|
| **BizBuySell** | Individual buyers, first-time acquirers, SBA borrowers | Highest volume; broadest audience; default for any sub-$5M listing |
| **BizQuest** | Similar to BizBuySell; slightly smaller volume | Secondary distribution; some buyers check only one |
| **DealStream** | Financial sponsors, search funds, PE firms | More sophisticated buyers; better for $1M+ deals |
| **LoopNet** | Real estate investors, RE-attached businesses | Only used when real property is included in the sale |
| **Axial** | Lower-middle-market PE, family offices, search funds | $2M+ EBITDA typically; invitation-based network |
| **Industry-specific boards** | Strategic buyers in the vertical | Trade associations, franchise resale networks, vertical SaaS deal boards |

### Listing Content Rules

- **Headline copy is the most important field** — the listing title in BizBuySell search results is the only thing most buyers read. It must contain: industry, revenue indicator, and a value hook ("Profitable HVAC Business — $800K Revenue, Recurring Contracts, Owner Retiring")
- **Anonymous by default** — same rules as the teaser. Name, address, and any identifying information are absent from the public listing.
- **NDA gate on financials** — financial details are never in the public listing. The listing shows revenue range and cash flow range; the full picture is gated behind NDA.
- **Keywords must match buyer search behavior** — brokers learn the search terms buyers use on each platform and optimize listings accordingly. "SBA pre-qualified," "seller financing available," "recurring revenue," "absentee owner" are high-click terms on BizBuySell.
- **Listing price alignment** — asking price in the listing must match the CIM. Any discrepancy between listing price and CIM price is an immediate trust problem with buyers.
- **Active management** — listings are not set-and-forget. Brokers update listings if they're not generating inquiries, test different headlines, adjust the description. A listing that's been live for 90 days with no inquiries needs to be revised or re-evaluated for price.
- **Syndication tracking** — brokers track which platforms are generating qualified inquiries vs. noise. Over time, most deals come from 1-2 sources; the others are maintained for coverage but de-prioritized.

## NDA / CDA Drafting — Deep Rules

The NDA is the legal instrument that gates access to confidential information. Brokers have strong opinions on what an NDA must contain.

### Mandatory NDA Clauses

- **Definition of confidential information** — must be broad enough to cover financials, customer lists, employee information, trade secrets, and the fact that the business is for sale. The last point is critical: the existence of the sale process is itself confidential.
- **Non-use clause** — the buyer agrees not to use the information for any purpose other than evaluating the acquisition. Without this, a competitor could use the CIM to poach customers or hire key employees.
- **Non-solicitation of employees** — buyer agrees not to recruit or hire any employee of the business for a specified period (typically 2 years). This is standard and non-negotiable for most sellers.
- **Non-solicitation of customers** — buyer agrees not to approach the seller's customers for their own business during the evaluation period and for a period post-termination.
- **Term** — most broker NDAs run 2-3 years from execution. Perpetual NDAs are often unenforceable.
- **Return or destruction of materials** — buyer must return or certify destruction of all confidential materials if the deal does not proceed.
- **Governing law and jurisdiction** — must specify the state whose law governs disputes. Brokers typically use the seller's state.
- **No-contact provision** — buyer agrees to channel all communication through the broker, not approach the seller directly. This protects the seller from being pressured or circumvented.

### Process Rules Around the NDA

- **NDA must be executed before any CIM or financial detail is shared** — no exceptions. Even verbally disclosing recast financials to an unqualified buyer before NDA execution is a broker error.
- **NDA tracking** — every executed NDA is logged with: buyer name, contact information, entity name if applicable, date of execution, and what was provided to them under the NDA.
- **NDA does not qualify the buyer** — execution of the NDA is the minimum bar, not the qualification bar. A signed NDA only unlocks the CIM. Financial qualification is a separate step that happens before the buyer packet is released.
- **Entity vs. individual signing** — if a buyer is representing a PE firm or search fund, the NDA must be signed by the entity, not just the individual. An individual signing on behalf of an entity they don't control creates an enforcement gap.

## Seller FAQ Document — Deep Rules

This document is underused by amateur brokers and essential for professionals. It exists because the same 15 questions come up in every deal, and if the seller has to answer them live, unprepared, in a buyer call, deals fall apart.

### The Questions Every FAQ Must Pre-Answer

**About the business:**
- Why are you selling?
- How long have you owned the business?
- What does a typical day look like for the owner?
- How many hours per week does the owner work?
- How dependent is the business on the owner?
- Who is the management team and will they stay post-close?

**About the financials:**
- Why is the recast revenue/SDE different from the tax return?
- What are the add-backs and why are they legitimate?
- What does revenue look like year-to-date vs. prior year?
- Are there any significant upcoming capital expenditures?

**About the deal:**
- Is the seller willing to provide financing?
- Is the business SBA eligible?
- What is included in the asking price? (inventory, A/R, equipment, real estate)
- What is the transition period?
- Will the seller sign a non-compete?

**About risk:**
- What are the key risks in this business?
- What happens to the customer relationships when the owner leaves?
- Are there any pending legal issues?

### FAQ Rules

- **Every answer must be pre-approved by the seller** — the broker drafts, the seller reviews and edits. A buyer will sometimes reference FAQ answers in the LOI or APA. If the seller gave a different answer verbally on a call, there's a conflict.
- **Answers must be honest, not marketing copy** — buyers are sophisticated. Evasive FAQ answers are worse than no FAQ at all because they signal the broker is hiding something. The FAQ is where you get ahead of weaknesses, not bury them.
- **FAQ evolves throughout the process** — every time a buyer asks a question that isn't in the FAQ, it gets added. The FAQ is a living document through the deal process.
- **FAQ is released with or after the full CIM** — never pre-NDA. Some brokers bundle the FAQ inside the CIM; others release it separately after the buyer has reviewed the CIM and submitted a question set.

## The Listing Prep Sequencing Rule

A broker has a strict build-order for these documents because each one derives from the prior:

```
Recast financials + seller sign-off
        ↓
CIM (full, identity-revealed version)
        ↓
Blind CIM / Teaser (strip identity from the full CIM — not a separate document)
        ↓
NDA (drafted in parallel, ready before first outreach)
        ↓
Marketplace listings (headline and description derived from CIM exec summary)
        ↓
Seller FAQ (drafted in parallel; finalized after seller reviews full CIM)
```

This sequence matters for Scorta's agent pipeline: the CIM generation agent is the root node. The teaser agent, marketplace listing agent, and FAQ agent are all derivative — they ingest the CIM and transform it. Only the NDA agent is truly independent, since it's a legal template with deal-specific variables filled in. That means your **CIM generation agent is the most architecturally critical piece of the entire brokerage product**.
