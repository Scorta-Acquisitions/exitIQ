What is your company going to make? Please describe your product and what it does or will do.
Scorta is an AI-native business brokerage: we sell the full service (not software) to owners of Main Street businesses selling for $500K–$5M. Our agent stack does everything a broker, CPA and business attorney does (recast, valuation, deal room, lender coordination, buyer outreach, close) for a $3–5K diagnostic + 5% at close, half of what brokers charge and every dollar figure is computed in deterministic code, traced to a source document.

A traditional broker puts ~(250 - 300) hours into a $1M deal. Our first close took <45 hours of human time.

Our second product, DealIQ (in build), gives sub-$5M buyers the same analysis from the other side of the table, and every buyer who signs up joins a capital-verified pool that lets our sell-side tell sellers, truthfully: "I have verified buyers looking for exactly your business right now."

Long term, Scorta is how every Main Street business in America gets sold.

Who are your competitors? What do you understand about your business that they don’t?
OffDeal : AI investment bank for $10–100M revenue businesses — their floor is ~$5M and we live below it on purpose. Baton: marketplace + human advisors; we're full-service and run the deal to close. Traditional brokers (Sunbelt, Transworld): 10–12% commission, minimal prep, list and pray. BizBuySell: ~50K live listings, 9,586 closed in 2025 just a listings wall. List any competitions/awards you have won, or papers you’ve published.
I worked with a research partner trying to generate a comprehensive meta-analysis on sternal reconstruction techniques. To do this rigorously, she needed to run nearly 700 distinct comparisons across different reconstructive strategies, each requiring separate effect size calculations, significance testing, and visualization. On paper the stats were straightforward, but at this scale the workflow was effectively intractable by hand-months of copy-pasting, re-running models, and manually checking outputs, with a constant risk of subtle errors creeping in.
I designed and built a custom analysis pipeline to take over this entire statistical layer. Starting from the analytical framework we defined, the system iterated through every planned comparison, computed the relevant meta-analytic estimates, evaluated statistical significance, and automatically discarded non-informative results. For the subset of meaningful findings, it generated publication-ready forest plots and ran structured robustness checks, including leave-one-out and leave-two-out sensitivity analyses across the included studies.
In practice, this turned what would have been an overwhelming, months-long manual process into a push-button workflow. The pipeline reliably executed ~700 meta-analyses across 75 studies and 4,465 patients-something that would have been nearly impossible to do exhaustively and consistently in spreadsheets—while reducing the surface area for human error. The resulting analyses helped clarify how timing and technique choices influence complications and pulmonary outcomes after sternal reconstruction, providing evidence that can directly inform surgical decision-making and protocol design in a high-risk patient population.
This work contributed to the abstract, “Comparison of Reconstruction Techniques, Surgical Timing, and Pulmonary Function Outcomes in Sternal Reconstruction: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis,” which has been accepted for a short podium presentation at the national Plastic Surgery Research Council (PSRC) annual meeting. For me, this project is a clear example of the kind of engineering I like to do: take a complex, high-value problem that doesn’t scale with human effort, and build tooling that makes the “impossible” version of the analysis not just feasible, but routine, and, in this case, capable of changing how care is delivered at the bedside.
